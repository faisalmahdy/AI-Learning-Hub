summary: An analyst who sees a strong correlation between X and Y is tempted to read a direction into it and act on it — "X and Y move together, so to raise Y, raise X." The observational data does not contain that direction. For a simple linear relationship the coefficient of determination R-squared equals the squared correlation regardless of which variable you regress on the other, so regressing Y on X and regressing X on Y fit the data identically: the fit is symmetric in the two variables, and both "X drives Y" and "Y drives X" explain the numbers exactly as well, which means the numbers pick neither. What carries direction is intervention. In a world where Y actually causes X, forcing X to a value externally leaves Y untouched (X is downstream, nothing feeds from X back to Y), while forcing Y changes X — and that asymmetry between the two experiments is the arrow, invisible in the correlation. So the naive reading is not merely unproven but actionably wrong: an analyst who concludes X→Y and intervenes on X to move Y finds Y does not move. On a fixture generated by the true model Y→X (X := 2·Y plus a small perturbation), the correlation is 0.9984 and the two regressions fit identically at R-squared 0.9968, yet forcing X has zero effect on Y while forcing Y changes X by 2 per unit — correlation earns you a relationship; only an intervention earns you a direction.
eli5: Imagine you notice that whenever the ice cream truck's music plays, kids appear on the street, and you conclude the music summons the kids — so you play the music at 3am to summon them, and nobody comes. The truck driver knew something you didn't: the driver plays the music because they see kids around (kids cause music), not the other way round. Just watching the two happen together cannot tell you which one makes the other happen, because "music brings kids" and "kids bring music" look exactly the same in a photo of a busy street. The only way to find out is to actually change one and see: force the music with no kids around, and if no kids come, the music was never the cause. Seeing two things move together tells you they are connected; it never tells you which one is the lever.

## Why this module

A correlation is a two-way street by construction: it measures how X and Y move together, and "together" has no arrow in it. Yet the moment we find one, the mind supplies a direction — usually the one we came in expecting — and then we reach for the lever: this metric predicts revenue, so let's push the metric. That last step assumes something the correlation never provided.

The neighboring modules in this topic handle a different confusion: a correlation that is real between X and Y but caused by a third variable (a confounder) or manufactured by conditioning on one (a collider). This module is about the case where X and Y genuinely are causally linked and the only question is which way the arrow points — X→Y or Y→X. The data cannot answer it, and that is not a limitation of your sample size; it is a fact about what a correlation is.

The reason is exact and worth stating: for a simple linear fit, the quality of the fit is identical whether you regress Y on X or X on Y, because both are governed by the same squared correlation. So "X predicts Y well" and "Y predicts X well" are the same statement, and neither is evidence for a causal direction. Acting on the wrong direction is not a subtle error — you pull a lever that isn't connected to anything and the outcome doesn't move.

**A correlation is symmetric in its two variables, and a simple linear fit is equally good in both directions, so the observational data cannot say whether X→Y or Y→X — and acting on the assumed direction can mean pulling a lever that does nothing.**

## Concepts

Picture the two candidate worlds. In one, X→Y: nudging X changes Y. In the other, Y→X: nudging Y changes X. Both worlds produce the same cloud of (X, Y) points with the same correlation, so a scatter plot — or any regression on it — is a photograph that both worlds could have produced. You cannot develop the photograph into an arrow.

What the two worlds do not share is how they respond to being pushed. This is the difference between seeing and doing. Seeing X high tells you Y is probably high in either world. Doing — forcing X to a value from outside — is different: it severs whatever normally sets X and imposes your value instead. In the X→Y world, forcing X still propagates to Y. In the Y→X world, forcing X hits a dead end, because the arrow runs the other way and nothing carries your change from X back to Y. The intervention distinguishes the worlds the correlation could not.

This is why observational data licenses a relationship but never a direction, and why the disciplines that establish direction are all about breaking the symmetry from outside the data: a randomized experiment (you assign X, so nothing upstream sets it), a temporal ordering (a cause cannot follow its effect), or structural knowledge (you know the mechanism). Absent one of those, "X is correlated with Y" leaves the arrow undetermined, and the safe statement is a relationship, not a lever.

**Seeing a value is symmetric between X and Y, but doing — forcing a variable, which severs what normally sets it — is not: the intervention that propagates in one direction and dead-ends in the other reveals the arrow, which is why direction needs an experiment, time order, or structural knowledge, never correlation alone.**

## Worked example

Source: faisalmahdy/ai-learning-hub — modules/ai-for-science-and-data/code/reversecause-inter-01. The fixture is generated by a known structural model in which Y causes X.

```json filename=modules/ai-for-science-and-data/code/reversecause-inter-01/reversecause.json:3-5 COMPLETE
  "y_values": [1, 2, 3, 4, 5],
  "slope": 2.0,
  "noise": [0.3, -0.2, 0.1, -0.1, 0.0]
```

The observed X is built from the true model — X is caused by Y.

```python filename=modules/ai-for-science-and-data/code/reversecause-inter-01/reversecause.py:32-34 COMPLETE
def observed_x(data):
    """The observational X, generated by the true model X := slope*Y + noise (Y -> X)."""
    return [data["slope"] * y + n for y, n in zip(data["y_values"], data["noise"])]
```

Forcing X cuts the edge that sets it; Y, being upstream, is untouched.

```python filename=modules/ai-for-science-and-data/code/reversecause-inter-01/reversecause.py:51-53 COMPLETE
def intervene_on_x(data, x_value):
    """do(X = x_value): cut the Y->X edge and set X. Y is exogenous, so it is unchanged."""
    return list(data["y_values"])          # Y does not depend on X; forcing X leaves Y as it is
```

Forcing Y flows down the intact arrow; X follows.

```python filename=modules/ai-for-science-and-data/code/reversecause-inter-01/reversecause.py:56-58 COMPLETE
def intervene_on_y(data, y_value):
    """do(Y = y_value): set Y; X is recomputed from the structural model X := slope*Y."""
    return data["slope"] * y_value         # X follows Y
```

Before running it, predict: X and Y will be strongly correlated, and the two regressions will fit identically, so the observational data will offer no way to tell that Y is the cause. Run `--observe`:

```text filename=reversecause.py --observe
OBSERVE — the observational data (true model is Y -> X)
--------------------------------------------------------
  Y = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
  X = [2.3, 3.8, 6.1, 7.9, 10.0]
  correlation(X, Y) = 0.9984
  R^2 regressing Y on X = 0.9968
  R^2 regressing X on Y = 0.9968
--------------------------------------------------------
  both regressions fit identically -- the data is symmetric, it names no direction
```

The prediction holds. The correlation is 0.9984 — as strong as data ever gets — and the two regressions have the identical R-squared of 0.9968. Regressing Y on X (the "X→Y" reading) and regressing X on Y (the "Y→X" reading) are equally good, to every decimal, because both are just the squared correlation. An analyst staring at this could tell any causal story they liked; the numbers endorse both and distinguish neither.

Now do, instead of see. Run `--intervene`:

```text filename=reversecause.py --intervene
INTERVENE — force one variable, see if the other moves
--------------------------------------------------------
  do(X=100) -> mean(Y) = 3.00
  do(X=200) -> mean(Y) = 3.00
  effect of forcing X on Y = 0.00  (X is downstream; Y does not move)
  do(Y=3) -> X = 6.00 ;  do(Y=4) -> X = 8.00
  effect of forcing Y on X (per unit) = 2.00  (Y is the true cause)
--------------------------------------------------------
  intervention is asymmetric where correlation was symmetric -- that asymmetry is the direction
```

The symmetry breaks the instant you push. Forcing X from 100 to 200 leaves the mean of Y at exactly 3.00 both times — a zero effect, because X is downstream and there is no path from it back to Y. Forcing Y, by contrast, moves X by 2 per unit, straight down the real arrow. The correlation said X and Y were joined at 0.9984; the intervention says the join runs one way only. An analyst who read "X→Y" from the correlation and pushed X to raise Y would have watched Y sit motionless — the lever was never connected.

## Build

The self-test plants the failure and names each claim as a boolean flag. It checks that X and Y are strongly correlated, that regressing each way fits identically, that forcing X has no effect on Y, that forcing Y does change X, and that the intervention is asymmetric though the correlation was symmetric.

```python filename=modules/ai-for-science-and-data/code/reversecause-inter-01/reversecause.py:100-114 COMPLETE
    corr_strong = abs(correlation(x, y)) > 0.9
    print("  X and Y are strongly correlated = %s (%.4f)" % (corr_strong, correlation(x, y)))

    fits_identical = abs(r_squared(x, y) - r_squared(y, x)) < 1e-12
    print("  regressing Y-on-X and X-on-Y fit identically = %s (%.4f == %.4f)" % (fits_identical, r_squared(x, y), r_squared(y, x)))

    eff_x_on_y = (sum(intervene_on_x(data, 200)) - sum(intervene_on_x(data, 100))) / len(y)
    forcing_x_does_not_move_y = abs(eff_x_on_y) < 1e-12
    print("  forcing X has no effect on Y = %s (effect %.4f)" % (forcing_x_does_not_move_y, eff_x_on_y))

    eff_y_on_x = intervene_on_y(data, 4) - intervene_on_y(data, 3)
    forcing_y_moves_x = abs(eff_y_on_x) > 0
    print("  forcing Y does change X = %s (effect %.2f per unit)" % (forcing_y_moves_x, eff_y_on_x))

    intervention_breaks_symmetry = forcing_x_does_not_move_y and forcing_y_moves_x
```

Run `--check`. It exits non-zero if any flag is false, so it fails loudly if the two fits ever diverged or the interventions ever became symmetric:

```text filename=reversecause.py --check
SELF-TEST — the two regressions fit equally (correlation gives no direction); intervening on X does not move Y while intervening on Y moves X
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  X and Y are strongly correlated = True (0.9984)
  regressing Y-on-X and X-on-Y fit identically = True (0.9968 == 0.9968)
  forcing X has no effect on Y = True (effect 0.0000)
  forcing Y does change X = True (effect 2.00 per unit)
  intervention is asymmetric though the correlation was symmetric = True
```

**The self-test asserts the fits are identical to twelve decimals and the interventions diverge — pinning the whole lesson in one pass: the observational data is exactly symmetric in the two variables (so it cannot carry a direction) while the intervention is exactly asymmetric (so only it can).**

## Boss fight

Suppose you cannot run an experiment — the data is observational — and you still need a direction. Reason about what can substitute. Two things sometimes can. Temporal precedence: if X is measured strictly before Y and the mechanism cannot reach back in time, X→Y is at least possible and Y→X is ruled out — though a common cause can still fake it, so time order narrows the arrows without fully fixing them. And instrumental variables or natural experiments: find something that perturbs X but could only affect Y through X (an "instrument"), which mimics an intervention on X using variation that already exists in the data — the basis of much of econometrics and Mendelian randomization in genetics. The lesson is that establishing direction always requires importing an asymmetry the correlation lacks, whether from a real experiment, the arrow of time, or an instrument; you never squeeze it out of the bivariate fit itself.

Now the trap that makes reverse causation especially seductive: it usually comes with a plausible story in the wrong direction. "Depressed people exercise less, so low exercise causes depression" — or does depression cause low exercise, or does a third factor cause both. The correlation fits every one of those, and the human tendency is to accept whichever direction matches a prior belief, then treat the correlation as confirmation. This is where reverse causation compounds with confounding (the neighboring module): the honest analysis lists all the causal structures consistent with the data — X→Y, Y→X, a common cause, or several at once — and reports that the data cannot choose among them, rather than picking the flattering one. A correlation is the beginning of a causal question, never the answer, and the discipline is to enumerate the arrows the data permits instead of asserting the one you hoped for.

**Direction can sometimes be recovered without a randomized experiment via temporal precedence or an instrumental variable, but each imports an asymmetry the correlation lacks — never the bivariate fit itself; and because a correlation fits X→Y, Y→X, and a common cause alike, the honest report enumerates all structures the data permits rather than asserting the plausible-sounding one.**
